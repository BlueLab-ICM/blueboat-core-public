The sensors below were chosen for specific research projects, not because the architecture requires them. They are worth describing because they show the range of what the platform absorbs — optical, acoustic, and radio — and because each one illustrates a different integration pattern.

## Optical: machine-vision camera

An Allied Vision Alvium U camera on a Sony IMX547 global-shutter sensor: 5.1 MP, 2.74 µm pixels, mounted inside a 2" Blue Robotics watertight enclosure on a mast about 50 cm above the deck. A printed bracket sets the tilt angle for the scenario.

<Note>
The global shutter is the part that matters. A rolling shutter on a boat produces skewed, smeared frames — a small vessel pitches and rolls continuously, and the distortion is worst exactly when the sea state is worst. Detection on a rolling-shutter image from a moving hull is a much harder problem than it needs to be.
</Note>

**Above water** the camera detects and localises small surface targets: pop-up buoys, marine wildlife, obstacles. In the field trials described in the paper, an onboard YOLO model produced 280 positive detections during a single run in which the vehicle repeatedly steered itself over a moored buoy, passing within 0.2 m of its known position — using vision alone, with the buoy's location never supplied to the boat.

**Below water** the same camera supports benthic habitat mapping, including seagrass (*Posidonia oceanica*) coverage assessment.

Integration: USB, on the Jetson, with inference in a payload container.

## Acoustic: modem and hydrophone

<CardGroup cols={2}>
  <Card title="SeaTrac X010 modem" icon="satellite-dish">
    Bidirectional underwater communication, ranging and tracking to roughly 1 km.

    Talks to seafloor instruments and AUVs while simultaneously providing range measurements — which is what makes range-based localisation of an underwater target from a surface vehicle possible.

    Integration: serial, via RS232-to-USB.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Cetacean Research C57" icon="waveform-lines">
    A preamplified broadband hydrophone covering infrasonic through ultrasonic.

    Passive acoustic monitoring of marine mammals, and detection of acoustic telemetry tags on animals.

    Integration: audio input, with processing in a payload.
  </Card>
</CardGroup>

The acoustic modem is the more interesting case architecturally: it is a sensor that produces *ranges to a moving target*, which is exactly the input an adaptive sampling algorithm needs. A payload consuming those ranges and publishing waypoints through `payload_waypoint` mode is a complete target-tracking system with no changes to the core stack.

## Integrating your own

The platform supports instruments through standard interfaces:

| Interface | Typical instruments |
|-----------|--------------------|
| USB | cameras, GPS receivers, serial adapters |
| Ethernet | high-bandwidth sensors, IP cameras, sonars |
| Serial (RS232-to-USB) | acoustic modems, CTDs, most oceanographic instruments |
| Coaxial (TNC) | antennas — six bulkhead connectors are fitted in the port-hull cover |

CTDs, ADCPs, multibeam echosounders and water-quality probes all fit this pattern without hardware or software changes beyond a driver payload.

### The integration pattern

<Steps>
  <Step title="Get it talking on the bench">
    Read the instrument from a plain Python script on the backseat computer, outside Docker. Confirm the wiring, the baud rate and the protocol before ROS 2 is anywhere near the problem.
  </Step>

  <Step title="Wrap it in a payload">
    Generate a repository from the [payload template](https://github.com/BlueLab-ICM/blueboat-payload-template) and move the read loop into a node that publishes under `/payload/<name>/`.

    Pass the device through to the container:

    ```yaml
    services:
      payload_ctd:
        devices:
          - "/dev/ttyUSB0:/dev/ttyUSB0"
    ```

    <Warning>
    `/dev/ttyUSB0` is assigned in enumeration order and will change when another USB device is plugged in — usually on the day it matters. Use a stable path from `/dev/serial/by-id/` instead.
    </Warning>
  </Step>

  <Step title="Prefer standard message types">
    `sensor_msgs` has well-defined types for most things: `Temperature`, `FluidPressure`, `Imu`, `PointCloud2`, `Image`. Using them means the rosbag, Foxglove and every other payload can read your data without your interface package.
  </Step>

  <Step title="Close the loop, if you want to">
    A payload that only records is useful. A payload that publishes to `/payload/waypoints/waypoint` steers the boat — with the geofence, the timeout and the staleness check applied on your behalf.

    [Adding a payload →](/guides/adding-a-payload)
  </Step>
</Steps>

## Practical notes

<AccordionGroup>
  <Accordion title="Power before compute" icon="bolt">
    Add up the draw before adding the instrument. A Jetson at 25 W plus a camera plus a modem is a real fraction of the energy budget, and endurance is usually what limits a survey rather than compute.
  </Accordion>

  <Accordion title="Save data inside the container's volume" icon="floppy-disk">
    Containers are ephemeral. Anything written outside a mounted volume disappears when the container is recreated, which will happen at the least convenient moment.

    ```yaml
    volumes:
      - ${MISSION_CAMERA_DIR:-./data}:/app/saved_data
    ```
  </Accordion>

  <Accordion title="Keep large data out of the rosbag" icon="database">
    Recording a camera stream fills a disk in minutes. Exclude it from the bag and have the payload write frames itself, at a rate you choose:

    ```bash
    # deployment/.env
    BAG_EXCLUDE_REGEX=^/payload/camera/image_raw$
    ```
  </Accordion>

  <Accordion title="Test in simulation first" icon="flask">
    A payload that reads `/frontseat/gps` and publishes waypoints can be developed entirely against the simulator, with the instrument replaced by synthetic data. Everything except the hardware driver is exercised on a laptop.
  </Accordion>
</AccordionGroup>
